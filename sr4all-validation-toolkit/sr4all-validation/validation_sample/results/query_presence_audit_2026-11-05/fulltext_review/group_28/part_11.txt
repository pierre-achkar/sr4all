e as that in the BRS group (33.52% vs. 17.68%), but, as a presenting symptom for intervention, BRS reported higher unstable angina/NSTEMI rates (35.86% vs. 23.56%) and less stable angina (50.43% vs. 71.02%) which, according to published literature, could translate into a more favourable outcome in the DBS group [24]. 4.2. Lesions’ Characteristics The DBS group had more true bifurcation lesions; alt-hough these lesions have a lower angiographic success rate, previous literature does not show a statistically significant correlation with worse clinical outcomes [25].   Both groups showed a similar prevalence of combined LAD/D and distal LMS lesions (BRS: 70.66%, DBS: 67%). These lesions are usually the culprit of stent thrombosis and other MACEs [26].   4.3. Clinical Outcomes The study at hand shows a statistically non-significant advantage of using BRS over DBS in all clinical outcomes except stent thrombosis. This advantage is not expected and thus warrants further research. The statistically non-significant elevated risk of stent thrombosis is in concord-ance with previously reported studies on scaffold use [27]. When the results of the six major trials of bifurcation le-sion stenting (Table 8) were weighed and compared to the outcomes of the two arms of the current study, the same trends were observed, with BRS failing to show advantage only in relation to stent thrombosis. The issue of scaffold thrombosis (ST) was first noted in the ABSORB randomised controlled trial, which reported nine cases of late ST [10]. This is thought to be due to the fact that BRS have low radial strength [28] and thus, are prone to fracture during post dilation [29,30]. Another con-tributing factor is the strut thickness, which can be as high as 170µm [11]. It is postulated that thicker struts generate high-er shear stress and, therefore, cause platelet activation [1]. Hernandez and Lee argued that advances in research would overcome these limitations [1].
