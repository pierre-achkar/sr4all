Introduction
Fracture reduction is a common and painful procedure in 
pediatric emergency care, requiring effective procedural 
sedation to ensure pain control, successful manipulation, 
and patient safety. Procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA) 
not only manage pain but also provide immobility and 
psychological comfort, which are essential for performing 
fracture reduction safely and effectively [ 1, 2]. Although

Abstract
Purpose of Review This review evaluates the safety and efficacy of ketamine, alone or in combination with other agents, 
for procedural sedation during fracture reduction in pediatric emergency settings. The goal is to provide clinicians with an 
updated synthesis of evidence to guide sedation strategies in this common clinical scenario.by synthesizing evidence on 
outcomes such as adverse events, sedation success, and recovery time.
Recent Findings A systematic review and meta-analysis of studies published up to January 2025 showed that the overall 
adverse event rate associated with ketamine was 24% (95% CI: 16–32%). Ketamine monotherapy was linked with higher 
rates of vomiting (13%) and agitation (17%), while combination regimens reduced agitation but were associated with a 
higher rate of severe hallucinations (28%). Airway problems were uncommon, with hypoxia occurring in only 1–5% of 
cases and no reports of intubation. Fracture reduction success rates were 86% with monotherapy and 99% with combination 
techniques. Recovery profiles differed: ketamine monotherapy had longer sedation duration (42.6 min) but shorter recovery 
time (44.1 min), while combinations resulted in shorter sedation (19.7 min) and longer recovery (76.7 min). Redosing was 
needed in 25% of monotherapy cases.
Summary Ketamine is a safe and effective option for procedural sedation in pediatric fracture reduction. While combination 
regimens enhance procedural success and reduce agitation, they are associated with longer recovery times and more severe 
hallucinations. Ketamine monotherapy offers a faster recovery but may require redosing. Clinicians should individualize 
sedation strategies based on patient characteristics and institutional capabilities. Further research is needed to optimize dos-
ing regimens, explore multimodal combinations, and conduct direct comparisons with other sedative agents.

notable differences in pediatric anesthetic services between 
nations and within healthcare facilities. In emergencies, 
ketamine is still a first-choice sedative because of its quick 
onset, strong analgesic effects, and capacity to preserve 
airway reflexes. Its appropriateness for reducing fractures 
in children has been the subject of continuous discussion, 
nevertheless, due to worries about circulatory instability, 
emergence phenomena, and possible pulmonary issues [ 5]. 
As a phencyclidine derivative, ketamine is usually prepared 
as a racemic combination in the form of hydrochloride salt, 
which can be rapidly redistributed [7, 8]. Ketamine is com-
monly utilized in pediatric emergency medicine for clinical 
purposes such as burn therapy, abscess drainage, joint dis -
location reduction, fracture manipulation, and foreign body 
removal [ 4]. By inducing a trance-like state, its dissocia -
tive qualities preserve hemodynamic stability and airway 
reflexes while producing extreme analgesia, forgetfulness, 
and immobility. Its sympathomimetic actions, however, 
have the potential to cause cardiovascular instability, such 
as marked hypertension and elevated heart rate, especially 
when given in large quantities or by fast intravenous infu -
sion [ 9]. Ketamine typically maintains respiratory drive 
and airway reflexes, but there have been isolated reports 
of respiratory depression and airway compromise [ 10, 11]. 
Its usage is made more difficult by neurological side effects 
such as emergent agitation, hallucinations, nystagmus, 
myoclonus, increased muscular tone, and hypersalivation, 
the latter of which occasionally calls for the complementary 
use of anticholinergic drugs like atropine or glycopyrrolate 
[12]. A patient’s unique traits, dosage, and the method of 
administration all affect how severe and frequent these side 
effects are [11].
This review addresses both critical evidence gaps and 
real-world challenges by specifically evaluating sedation 
outcomes during fracture reduction, a procedure with dis -
tinct sedation demands and wide practice variability com -
pared to other pediatric interventions. To address this gap, 
we conducted a rigorous systematic review and meta-anal -
ysis focused specifically on the safety and effectiveness of 
ketamine monotherapy or in combination during pediatric 
fracture reduction in emergency settings with a specific 
focus on adverse effects, including respiratory depression, 
emergence reactions, and cardiovascular instability. By syn-
thesizing available evidence, this study aims to guide clini -
cal decision-making and highlight areas for further research 
regarding the optimal use of ketamine in pediatric bone 
reduction.
Methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted 
following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic 
Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [ 13] 
and methodological guidance from the Cochrane Handbook 
for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [14]. The protocol 
was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database 
under registration ID: CRD245456 to ensure methodologi -
cal transparency and minimize bias throughout the review 
process.
Literature Search
A comprehensive literature search was conducted on four 
major biomedical databases comprising Web of Science, 
PubMed, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Tri -
als (CENTRAL), and Embase. The search approach used 
keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms 
such as “Ketamine,” “Pediatric,” “Bone Reduction,” “Frac-
ture,” “Emergency Department,” “Sedation,” and “Safety” 
with Boolean Operators “AND” and “OR” to further nar -
row down the search results. The search was restricted to 
articles published in English up to January 2025. Additional 
relevant studies were manually filtered from the reference 
lists of included articles and pertinent systematic reviews to 
guarantee a thorough research selection.
Eligibility Criteria
Studies were eligible for inclusion if they investigated 
pediatric patients, aged 0–18 years, undergoing procedural 
sedation with ketamine as the main sedative or analgesic, 
regardless of dosage or mode of administration, either as 
monotherapy or in combination with adjunct medications 
for bone/fracture reduction surgeries performed in emer -
gency departments, tertiary care institutions, pediatric 
trauma centers, or other acute pediatric care settings. We 
included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective 
or retrospective cohort studies, and observational studies
